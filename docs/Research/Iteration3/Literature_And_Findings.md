# Borrowed Eyes — Integrated Literature Review and Research Findings

**DECO3500 | Iteration 3 | Research synthesis**  
**Scope:** Ten previously used and newly selected academic sources. This document integrates earlier research with the current Solo, Borrowed Eyes, and Blind Eyes concepts. It does **not** replace the chronological records of earlier literature searches.

## 1. Research Focus

Our design opportunity is to use artworks as shared conversation anchors that invite visitors to exchange perspectives, share personal associations, understand different viewpoints, and explore opportunities for meaningful human connection.

This review asks four questions:

1. How do visitors interpret artworks and use other people's perspectives?
2. How can interactive museum experiences encourage participation without losing attention to the exhibit?
3. How can different information and perspectives lead to communication, coordination, and cooperation?
4. What design and evaluation limits should we recognise when investigating mutual understanding and social connection?

## 2. Selected Literature (10 Sources)

### L1. Shaer et al. (2011) — ARTeMuse: prompting active interpretation

**Source and method:** Shaer and colleagues presented the design, development, and evaluation of ARTeMuse, a social-mobile museum application aimed at beginning art viewers.

**Literature finding:** Short questions and structured activities can provide starting points for engagement and reflection. The evaluation reported greater engagement among beginning viewers using the application than in conventional viewing.

**Design implication:** An artwork fragment or concise question can start a visitor's response without requiring expert knowledge. In Solo and Blind Eyes, prompts should encourage looking and articulation rather than demand a single 'correct' personal interpretation.

**Limit:** This paper does not establish that prompts automatically create interpersonal connection or cooperation.

**Source:** [Art App-reciation: Fostering Engagement and Reflection in Museums through a Social-Mobile Application](https://cs.wellesley.edu/~oshaer/Art_App.pdf).

### L2. Yi et al. (2022) — Visitor-based social contextual information (VSCI)

**Source and method:** An art-museum study combining an initial survey, mobile-prototype design, and experimental evaluation of visitor-generated contextual information.

**Literature finding:** Visitors could compare their reactions with others and encounter alternative approaches to appreciating artworks when supplied with visitor-based social contextual information.

**Design implication:** The Solo experience's earlier visitors' comments and the newer perspective-sharing activities should present another visitor's view as an *interpretive resource*, not an authoritative answer. Allow participants to form an initial impression and then compare it with another person's view.

**Limit:** Encountering someone else's opinion is not equivalent to reciprocal understanding of that person.

**Source:** [PLOS ONE, 17(5), e0266856](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0266856).

### L3. Eklund (2020) — Interpersonal meaning-making in museums

**Source and method:** An ethnographic museum study combining observation and extended focus-group interviews on visitors' social interaction and meaning-making.

**Literature finding:** Meaning-making can emerge through visitors' shared knowledge, play, movement, and references to familiar experience. Museum interpretation is not solely an individual encounter with information.

**Design implication:** Borrowed Eyes should pay attention to *how* participants react to each other's ideas and personal associations, not just whether they reach an artwork identification.

**Limit:** Observations of existing museum social interaction do not establish the effects of our specific game rules or stranger matching.

**Source:** [A Shoe Is a Shoe Is a Shoe](https://doi.org/10.1080/10447318.2020.1767982).

### L4. Ryding et al. (2021) — Interpersonalising museum experiences

**Source and method:** Critical reflection on the deployed *Gift* and *Never Let Me Go* museum experiences, in which visitors shape another person's encounter with exhibits.

**Literature finding:** Designing opportunities for one person to influence another's museum experience can make interpretation relational and personally meaningful; the work also highlights the particular role of intimacy and relationships in those experiences.

**Design implication:** Retain space for visitor-created observations and personal associations across modes. The contribution should be meaningful beyond simply delivering the next factual clue.

**Limit:** The study's interpersonal experiences cannot be used to claim that a brief interaction between strangers produces lasting connection.

**Source:** [Interpersonalizing Intimate Museum Experiences](https://doi.org/10.1080/10447318.2020.1870829).

### L5. Hornecker & Stifter (2006) — Group engagement with public installations

**Source and method:** Evaluation of interactive installations in a technical museum, combining usage logs, observations and visitor interviews.

**Literature finding:** The physical arrangement and affordances of an installation shape who participates and how groups gather and interact. Hands-on, active experiences could attract different visitor groups; interaction barriers could discourage engagement.

**Design implication:** Keep onboarding simple and make participation roles and transitions legible. Observe people actually using the gallery space rather than relying only on their retrospective opinions.

**Limit:** The evaluated setting was a technical museum, not Borrowed Eyes' art-gallery game; it does not establish that our three modes are equally accessible.

**Source:** [Learning from Interactive Museum Installations About Interaction Design for Public Settings](https://doi.org/10.1145/1228175.1228201). **User-provided PDF:** pages 1–5 describe evaluation approach and examples of engagement and usability barriers.

### L6. Pilz et al. (2026) — Visitor types and interpretive label design

**Source and method:** A Rijksmuseum mixed-methods study comparing interpretive label conditions and visitor types, using eye tracking, interviews and self-report measures.

**Literature finding:** Visitors' experience and engagement with labels differ by visitor type and label framing. Interpretive material influences how and where visitors allocate attention, but effects depend on audience and context.

**Design implication:** Provide an accessible entry point for less-experienced visitors while allowing different degrees of engagement. Avoid assuming one prompt, one mode or one explanation works identically for everyone.

**Limit:** The study evaluated museum labels about historically sensitive art, not paired games, teamwork or social bonding.

**Source:** [Reading between the lines: How visitor type and label design shape engagement with art in the museum](https://doi.org/10.1007/s41809-026-00203-2). **User-provided PDF:** abstract and methods, pp. 181–185.

### L7. Hawkins, Gweon & Goodman (2021) — Visual information asymmetry

**Source and method:** Computational framework plus two behavioural experiments about speakers' and listeners' perspective-taking when access to visual information differs.

**Literature finding:** Speakers adjusted how informative their descriptions were according to what they knew about a partner's restricted visual perspective; listeners also adapted to communication difficulties over time.

**Design implication:** Blind Eyes should let the Describer respond to questions and clarify descriptions rather than rely exclusively on automatic hints. Test whether misunderstandings lead to useful reformulation.

**Limit:** The study examines controlled communication tasks, not art interpretation or whether a factual-question cap is effective.

**Source:** [Cognitive Science, 45(3), e12926](https://doi.org/10.1111/cogs.12926).

### L8. Tolmie et al. (2014) — Coordination in museum group visits

**Source and method:** Ethnographic work on group museum visiting and the social organisation of visiting practices.

**Literature finding:** Group visits involve practical coordination of attention, movement and participation. Individual interests and group progress do not always align, and members can be pulled away from exhibits or left out.

**Design implication:** In Face-to-Face/team play, examine who proposes a search direction, how disagreements are resolved, whether anyone is rushed, and whether all members can contribute before a shared guess.

**Limit:** The study describes naturally occurring group visits rather than testing our information-distribution mechanic.

**Source:** [Supporting Group Interactions in Museum Visiting, CSCW 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2531602.2531619).

### L9. Fosh, Benford & Koleva (2016) — Flexible group coherence

**Source and method:** Design and study of a museum visiting experience addressing how groups remain socially connected while navigating exhibits and responding individually.

**Literature finding:** Group coherence can be flexible and configurable; successful collective visits need not require everyone to act in lockstep at every moment.

**Design implication:** Team activities should allow different members to notice and offer different things, instead of forcing identical paths and equal speaking time. The group should be able to integrate diverse interpretations during a shared decision.

**Limit:** Group coherence is not identical to deep mutual understanding, and results should not be generalised automatically to newly matched strangers.

**Source:** [Supporting Group Coherence in a Museum Visit, CSCW 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2818048.2819970).

### L10. Bréhault et al. (2025) — Dimensions of asymmetry in collaboration

**Source and method:** Systematic literature review of 46 papers describing 57 collaborative systems, classifying eight dimensions of interaction asymmetry.

**Literature finding:** Collaborative experiences can differ across content access, input/output modalities and other dimensions; the review offers a vocabulary for identifying these asymmetries and associated challenges. It does **not** conclude that making information unequal is always better.

**Design implication:** In Blind Eyes, roles control different visual information. In Team Mode, clues are distributed across members. Document exactly which information differs, what feedback participants share, and whether the information distribution supports genuine contributions or causes exclusion.

**Limit:** This review is a classification framework rather than an efficacy study of Borrowed Eyes or a gallery-based comparison.

**Source:** [CHI 2025, Article 704](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713129).

## 3. Synthesised Literature Findings

The following are **cross-paper syntheses** produced for this project; they should not be represented as quotations or as experimental outcomes from a single paper.

| Finding | Literature basis | Design implication | Question for Borrowed Eyes evaluation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **LF1. Visitors have different interpretive needs and participation styles.** | L1, L5, L6 | Offer an easy starting point and flexible forms of participation; retain Solo rather than forcing real-time interaction. | Do participants understand how to start and choose a comfortable mode? |
| **LF2. Other visitors' perspectives can expand interpretation.** | L2, L3, L4 | Support personal interpretations and opportunities to encounter, explain or respond to others' views. | Does a participant notice, question or reconsider something after another perspective? |
| **LF3. Different information creates a need for communicative adaptation, not guaranteed understanding.** | L7, L10 | Provide role-aware questioning, description and feedback in Blind Eyes; share distinct clues in teams. | Do participants clarify misunderstandings, and do contributions alter the search? |
| **LF4. Coordination is part of collective museum visiting.** | L5, L8, L9 | Allow groups to negotiate routes, attention, participation and decisions rather than assuming that group formation is sufficient. | Who influences the plan, who waits, and how are disagreements resolved? |
| **LF5. Collaboration must build on individual contributions.** | L3, L9, L10 | Require synthesis of members' clues and interpretations, not just simultaneous searching. | Can members explain which contributions changed the shared decision? |
| **LF6. Active engagement should remain connected to the artwork and gallery setting.** | L1, L5, L6 | Keep prompts short, make physical looking central, and evaluate both interaction and artwork attention. | Do visitors look back at the art, or focus primarily on the interface and puzzle? |

## 4. Connection to the Current Design

### Solo — earlier Borrowed Eyes experience (Iteration 2)

Existing Solo testing in Iteration 2 should remain part of the evidence history. L1–L4 and L6 provide a background for looking closely, interpreting independently, encountering other visitors' views and contributing a perspective. **Do not claim these publications validate Solo's exact sequence or prove that asynchronous exchange creates interpersonal connection.**

### Blind Eyes — communication across information differences

L7 and L10 support the research rationale for different visual access and adaptive communication. L2–L4 support keeping interpretations and personal associations central rather than turning interaction into a purely factual guessing exercise. The current design's attempts and clue-unlock rules are **design decisions**, not findings confirmed by these papers.

### Face-to-Face / team-based Borrowed Eyes — negotiation and cooperation

L5, L8 and L9 ground research into real group behaviour in exhibitions. L10 supplies a framework for describing unequal clue access. L3–L4 remind us to examine how different subjective readings contribute to the group, not merely whether the group wins.

## 5. Research Gaps and Next Questions

1. **Self-disclosure and connection:** These ten papers collectively support perspective-sharing and interpersonal museum experiences, but they do not directly establish that strangers will share personal experiences or form meaningful relationships in a brief Borrowed Eyes session.
2. **Meaningful exchange vs task success:** A correct guess is not sufficient evidence of understanding another person's reasons or background. A post-result comparison and follow-up questions may help test this.
3. **Equal participation:** Different clues alone do not guarantee every member feels able to contribute. Observe acknowledgement, interruptions, ignored evidence and who decides.
4. **Digital implementation:** The planned online matching, messaging, location checks and publishing flows need their own functionality, accessibility, privacy and field-use validation.
