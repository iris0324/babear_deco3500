# Borrowed Eyes — Prototype Testing Results

**Project:** DECO3500 Social & Mobile Computing — Borrowed Eyes  
**Testing date:** 14 October 2026  
**Study type:** Small formative prototype evaluation  
**Activities:** Four Blind Eyes rounds and one Face-to-Face session

## 1. Aim and scope
We investigated whether collaborative artwork-finding tasks could create opportunities for exchanging interpretations, rather than merely identifying the target artwork. Task performance and social interaction were considered separately.

Four people took part across four two-player Blind Eyes rounds, with participants sometimes switching Eye and Finder roles. These are not eight independent participants. One Face-to-Face session involved three people who also participated in Blind Eyes; the two activities therefore used overlapping participants.

The Blind Eyes evidence included observation counts and post-test interviews. Face-to-Face findings relied mainly on post-test interviews because the observation count fields were blank.

## 2. Blind Eyes — mechanism
The **Eye** knew the target artwork. The **Finder** asked questions to identify it. The Finder could ask **up to three factual questions** and **unlimited perspective questions**. The information asymmetry was intended to invite discussion of feelings, meaning, and personal interpretations.

### 2.1 Recorded observations

| Metric | Test 01 | Test 02 | Test 03 | Test 04 | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Factual questions | 2 | 3 | 3 | 2 | **10** |
| Perspective initiations | 2 | 5 | 8 | 2 | **17** |
| Interpretive explanations | 2 | 5 | 8 | 2 | **17** |
| Substantive responses | 2 | 5 | 10 | 4 | **21** |
| Perspective differences | 1 | 4 | 5 | 0 | **10** |
| Newly noticed details | 2 | 4 | 6 | 2 | **14** |

All four rounds contained perspective initiations, interpretive explanations, and substantive responses. These event counts show observable interaction but do not by themselves demonstrate depth, mutual understanding, or the quality of the exchange. Some records of follow-up and communication repair were missing and have not been treated as zero.

### 2.2 Findings by round

**Test 01 — Curiosity from different interpretations.** The Eye described a woman looking in a mirror during an everyday activity, while the Finder interpreted her as checking her body or appearance. The Finder anticipated this difference and expressed interest in discussing it after the reveal. This suggests that contrasting interpretations could motivate discussion beyond task completion.

**Test 02 — Subjective clues created both direction and ambiguity.** An unsettling atmosphere directed the Finder toward paintings with tense expressions. The Finder had encountered the target but did not initially choose it. After the reveal, participants also disagreed about the number of figures. Ambiguity helped drive the search but also became a basis for comparison.

**Test 03 — Frequent interaction did not remove task focus.** The Eye emphasised beauty and harmony between people and nature, while the Finder considered religion and teaching. This round had the highest counts for perspective initiations and responses, yet the Finder described mainly using elimination to search. High event frequency should not be equated with deep reflection.

**Test 04 — Quick discovery limited discussion during the search.** The Finder used colour, landscape, and abstraction; the Eye described sadness, emptiness, and associations with ancient civilisations. The work was located quickly, leaving limited time for discussion. The interview identified interpretive differences, although the observation sheet recorded zero perspective differences. Both sources should be retained without silently reconciling them.

### 2.3 Cross-round findings

1. **The factual-question limit may prompt more selective factual questioning.** Participants stayed within the three-question limit and some described considering subjective questions. Since no unlimited-question comparison was tested, this is not proof that the rule caused more meaningful discussion.
2. **Perspective-related questions could still function as search tools.** Asking about mood or meaning sometimes narrowed down possible artworks instead of producing reciprocal exploration of another person's interpretation.
3. **The artwork reveal offers a promising moment for reflection.** Once both people could see the same artwork, differences in action, atmosphere, features, and symbolic meanings became easier to discuss. The evidence does not always establish which exchanges occurred spontaneously versus during interviews.
4. **The Eye's contribution could be constrained by the guessing format.** Some Eyes hesitated to give additional explanation without a question or feared revealing the answer.
5. **Participants reported some positive social experiences, but comfort varied.** One Eye grew more comfortable over time. Anonymity was not independently manipulated and should not be claimed as the cause.

## 3. Face-to-Face — mechanism and findings
Three participants received different perspective clues and explored artworks together without fixed roles. The reported sequence was: **share clues → explore candidate artworks → compare possibilities → make a collective choice**.

**Emergent coordination, but no verified stable role division.** All participants reported exchanging clues and discussing candidate artworks. P1 recalled dividing the search spatially, while P2 and P3 did not recall an explicit division. This supports self-organised exploration, not a stable role assignment agreed by everyone.

**Negotiating the meaning of clues.** Participants disagreed about descriptions such as “cool colour” and whether particular works matched. Such negotiation encouraged explanation and comparison, but disagreement about clue definitions is not necessarily deep artwork interpretation.

**Other contributions influenced choices.** P1 said teammates' reasoning led them to reject some candidates. P2 reported acquiring new ways of viewing works through others' explanations. Participants therefore did more than simply exchange independent clues.

**Unequal comfort remained possible.** P1 felt comfortable expressing disagreement. P2, who described being introverted, found speaking up difficult at times, although they did contribute. Collective task completion should not be treated as evidence of equal participation.

**Physical context mattered.** P3 described the exhibition area as small, while P2 had prior familiarity with some artworks. These conditions may have shaped navigation and coordination.

**Task outcome:** The supplied task-outcome field was blank. The interviews describe choosing an artwork, but the data do **not** confirm that the group selected the correct target. No success rate can be reported.

## 4. Cross-mode interpretation

| Dimension | Blind Eyes | Face-to-Face |
|---|---|---|
| Distribution of information | The Eye knows the target; the Finder asks questions | Each person begins with different clues |
| Main observed/reported interaction | Perspective questions, explanations, responses, interpretive differences | Clue sharing, negotiation, coordination, joint decision-making |
| Key limitation | Interpretation may serve only to narrow down the target | Discussion may remain about matching clues rather than artwork meaning |
| Participation issue | Eyes sometimes held back explanations | Quieter members may find disagreement difficult |
| Evaluation strength | Quantified observation plus interviews | Interview accounts; incomplete structured observation |

Both modes created opportunities for social interaction, but **question-asking, collaborative search, and meaningful perspective exchange are different outcomes**. These data do not establish which mode is more effective.

## 5. Design implications for the next iteration

1. **Keep testing the core interactions.** Preserve the three-factual-question cap as a hypothesis to evaluate, not as a demonstrated effective rule. Continue exploring flexible roles and complementary clues.
2. **Add a short, optional post-reveal comparison prompt to test.** Ask each participant for an initial view, a similarity or difference with another view, and something they would now look at again.
3. **Clarify when Eyes may explain proactively.** Prevent accidental disclosure of the target while allowing richer interpretation.
4. **Separate search-stage and reveal-stage observations.** Record when questions help identification and when they lead to interpretation or reconsideration.
5. **Track influence, not only talk frequency.** Note whether a contribution prompts follow-up, changes a decision, or changes what someone notices.
6. **Observe team coordination directly.** For Face-to-Face, document suggestions, responses, agreement, and subsequent actions.
7. **Provide room for quieter participants.** Test ways to invite contributions without making everyone speak the same amount.

These are **proposed changes**, not implemented or validated features.

## 6. Methodological limitations

- Small formative study, repeated participation, and overlap between modes.
- Missing Blind Eyes observation fields and occasional disagreement between observation sheets and interview accounts.
- Missing Face-to-Face observation counts and task outcome; no reliable event totals or completion accuracy.
- Some insights were elicited by the researcher's interview prompts rather than directly observed during gameplay.
- No controlled comparison of modes, anonymity, or factual-question limits; causal conclusions are unsupported.

## 7. Conclusion
The testing suggests that Borrowed Eyes can prompt exchanges of observations and interpretations while participants search for art. Blind Eyes made personal differences visible, whereas Face-to-Face highlighted communication, negotiation, and collective decision-making. The central next-step challenge is to support a transition from task-oriented information sharing to reciprocal understanding, without marginalising quieter visitors.
