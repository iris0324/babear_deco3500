# Interview Round 1 — Gallery Visitor Experiences

> **Project:** Increasing Art Gallery Involvement through Social and Mobile Computing (SOMO)  
> **Research location:** Queensland Art Gallery and Gallery of Modern Art (QAGOMA), South Bank, Brisbane  
> **Participants:** 5 gallery visitors  
> **Research focus:** Interpretation, conversation, social connection, and mobile interaction in physical art galleries

## Project Context 

This project explores how social and mobile computing could increase visitor involvement in physical art galleries. The early research focused on casual and inexperienced visitors: people who may be interested in art but do not always have the knowledge, confidence, or conversational support needed to interpret and discuss unfamiliar artworks.

The interviews examined how visitors currently look at artworks, use gallery information, talk with companions, respond to other visitors' perspectives, and feel about mobile interaction in a gallery. The aim was to identify design opportunities that could support engagement without making the phone the centre of the visit.

## Research Role 

Binyan Zhang led the user research and helped the team understand gallery visitors' experiences, behaviours, needs, and values through interviews, contextual observations, and analysis. This included organising the interview evidence, identifying repeated themes, and translating the findings into design implications.

## Research Method 

Round 1 used five short, semi-structured interviews at QAGOMA. The interview records included basic participant information, visiting style, a short contextual observation, open-ended questions, and follow-up questions. The questions differed slightly between participants so the interviewer could respond to each person's situation, such as visiting alone, visiting with a friend, making a first visit, visiting with a child, or visiting occasionally.

The analysis compared repeated behaviours and concerns across the five records. Similar responses were grouped into themes about interpretation, conversation, indirect social connection, and digital participation. The interview guide below is a retrospective consolidation of questions that appear in the existing records. It is not a claim that every participant was asked every question in exactly the same wording.

## Interview Questions

The following 12-question guide combines and standardises the questions found across the five interviews.

1. **How often do you visit art galleries, and who do you usually visit with?**
2. **What do you normally do when you first look at an artwork?**
3. **Do you usually feel confident interpreting artworks? Why or why not?**
4. **What do you do when you see an artwork you do not understand?**
5. **Do artwork labels or other gallery information help you understand the artwork? Is the information easy to understand?**
6. **When you visit with someone, do you usually talk about the artworks? What helps or limits the conversation?**
7. **Would a short question or prompt help you observe or discuss an artwork? What kind of prompt would be useful?**
8. **Would you be interested in seeing how other visitors interpreted the same artwork? What types of responses would you want to see?**
9. **Would you feel comfortable sharing your own interpretation? Would anonymity affect your decision?**
10. **Would you feel comfortable communicating directly with strangers in the gallery? Would indirect or anonymous interaction feel different?**
11. **What type of mobile interaction would feel comfortable and enjoyable during a gallery visit?**
12. **What would discourage you from using a mobile gallery activity?**

## Interview Evidence 

The evidence below is selected from the existing interview records. It is brief so that readers can connect participant responses to the later analysis.

### P1 — Solo international student

- She looked at the artwork first and read the label when she did not understand it.
- She had emotional reactions but said, **“I don't know whether my interpretation makes sense.”**
- Professional language made her feel that there was a correct meaning she was expected to understand.
- She wanted to see short responses from other visitors, especially to learn whether someone felt the same way.
- She might post anonymously and preferred a short question, a few choices, or one sentence.
- She rejected long reading and extended phone use: **“I came here to look at the artworks.”**

### P2 — Visitor attending with a friend

- He and his friend made comments such as **“That looks strange”** and **“I like that one,”** but did not develop deeper discussions.
- He explained, **“We don't really know what questions to ask.”**
- Long labels lost his attention after the first few lines.
- A specific prompt about a detail or mood could give them something to discuss.
- Comparing answers with his friend sounded enjoyable, but he wanted only a small number of interesting responses from strangers.
- Direct communication with strangers felt awkward; an anonymous reaction felt acceptable.
- He wanted the activity to be quick, playful, and unlike a test.

### P3 — First-time gallery visitor

- She sometimes did not know what she was **“supposed to look at.”**
- When she did not understand an artwork, she watched her friends, took a photograph, read the title, or moved on.
- She felt that other people understood something she did not and avoided sharing opinions that might sound **“silly.”**
- A simple prompt to find a detail or choose an emotion could help her know where to start.
- She did not want correct-answer feedback because it would feel like a quiz.
- Anonymous perspectives were interesting, but she did not want to be automatically matched for a meeting.
- Account creation, personal information, and long responses would discourage participation.

### P4 — Parent visiting with a child

- She used questions such as what the child could see or which part he liked, but had to create these prompts herself.
- She did not always feel confident explaining artworks and tried to simplify labels.
- Long, adult-focused explanations lost the child's attention.
- She supported one short shared activity, such as finding a hidden detail or choosing emotions.
- Comparing responses could begin a conversation because children may notice different things.
- She raised privacy concerns about displaying a child's name, image, or personal information.
- She wanted each activity to take less than two minutes and direct attention back to the artwork.

### P5 — Older occasional visitor

- Historical background helped him, and he usually read the label after first looking at the artwork.
- He did not scan a QR code because its destination was unclear and he did not want to download an app or register.
- He believed ordinary visitors sometimes explained reactions more clearly than formal gallery text.
- He might select an option but would not type much on his phone.
- He did not want direct connection with another visitor, but might leave an anonymous response or react to a comment.
- He wanted moderation of irrelevant or inappropriate content.
- A valuable activity would reveal something new and encourage him to look at the artwork again.

## Thematic Analysis 主题分析

### Theme 1 — Limited confidence in interpreting unfamiliar art

P1 questioned whether her interpretation made sense. P3 did not know what she was expected to notice and worried about saying something silly. P4 also lacked confidence when explaining artworks. These responses suggest that interest is not the main problem. Visitors may disengage because they are unsure how to begin or believe there is one correct interpretation.

**Finding:** Casual and inexperienced visitors may be interested in artworks but lack confidence in beginning and expressing an interpretation.

### Theme 2 — Visitors need a conversation trigger

P2 and his friend already made social comments, but their discussion stopped because they did not know what to ask next. P4 used simple observation questions to involve her child. P3 also believed a prompt about a detail or emotion could provide a starting point.

**Finding:** The barrier is not always an unwillingness to talk. A specific, artwork-centred prompt can help visitors move beyond simple likes and dislikes.

### Theme 3 — Social connection without direct stranger interaction

P1 wanted to compare feelings with others but preferred anonymity. P2 accepted anonymous reactions but found direct conversation awkward. P3 wanted to see anonymous perspectives without meeting anyone automatically. P5 also preferred leaving or reacting to an anonymous response rather than being directly connected.

**Finding:** Visitors may want social connection without direct face-to-face interaction with strangers.

### Theme 4 — Lightweight digital interaction

Across the records, participants preferred short questions, limited choices, brief written responses, comparisons, and simple activities. They did not want long reading, account creation, personal-data entry, unclear QR codes, app downloads, or long comments. P4 suggested less than two minutes for an activity.

**Finding:** Digital participation should be quick, optional, easy to understand, and low effort.

### Theme 5 — Digital support should return attention to the artwork

P1 did not want to spend a long time looking at her phone. P4 wanted the activity to direct the parent and child back to the artwork. P5 valued technology only when it revealed something new and encouraged another look.

**Finding:** Technology is useful when it strengthens attention to the physical artwork, not when the screen becomes the main experience.

### Theme 6 — Gallery information helps but can create barriers

P1 felt that professional language suggested a correct answer. P2 stopped reading long labels. P4 had to simplify adult-focused text for a child. In contrast, P5 valued historical background but also found ordinary visitors' reactions easier to understand than formal text.

**Finding:** Expert information remains useful, but it should support personal interpretation rather than act as the only entry point.

## Key Findings 

1. **Visitors can be interested in art while lacking interpretation confidence.** They need an accessible starting point, not a correct answer.
2. **Short prompts can trigger richer observation and conversation.** Visitors may already want to talk but lack a useful question.
3. **Visitors are curious about other perspectives but prefer low-pressure social contact.** Anonymous and indirect exchange is more acceptable than direct stranger communication.
4. **Mobile interaction must remain lightweight.** A useful activity should require little reading, minimal typing, no forced account, and clear instructions.
5. **The physical artwork must remain central.** Digital interaction should lead visitors to observe again rather than hold their attention on the screen.
6. **Trust and content quality matter.** Privacy, minimal personal data, and comment moderation are important, especially where children or public responses are involved.

## Design Implications 

| Research evidence | Design implication |
|---|---|
| Visitors do not know where to begin interpreting an artwork. | Provide one short, artwork-specific observation or emotion prompt. Avoid presenting one response as the correct answer. |
| Conversations often stop at “I like it” or “It looks strange.” | Use prompts, separate responses, or comparisons to give companions a clear conversation trigger. |
| Visitors want other perspectives but avoid direct contact with strangers. | Support anonymous, indirect, and asynchronous sharing, such as viewing selected responses or leaving a simple reaction. Do not automatically connect people. |
| Participants prefer short and simple interaction. | Keep each activity brief, ideally within the recorded preference of less than two minutes. Use taps, choices, swipes, or an optional one-sentence response. |
| Accounts, app downloads, personal information, unclear QR codes, and long input create barriers. | Make entry clear and immediate. Avoid required registration, unnecessary data collection, and long-form responses. |
| Large comment feeds may create overload and moderation problems. | Show a small, relevant set of perspectives rather than hundreds of comments. Include content management and reporting safeguards. |
| Participants do not want the phone to dominate the visit. | Structure the experience as: look at the artwork → complete a short prompt → see another perspective → look back at the artwork. |
| Formal labels are sometimes useful but can feel long or academic. | Keep expert information available as optional support while allowing visitors to form a personal response first. |

## Refined Research Insight 

The interviews suggest that casual and inexperienced gallery visitors do not necessarily lack interest in art. Instead, they often lack confidence, conversational support, and accessible ways to form and share interpretations. Participants were interested in seeing other visitors' perspectives, but they preferred anonymous and low-pressure interaction rather than direct contact with strangers. They also wanted digital activities to remain short and to redirect attention to the physical artwork.

**Refined design opportunity:** How might we help visitors discover and exchange different interpretations of artworks through lightweight social interaction, without distracting attention from the physical artwork?

## Limitations 

- This round included only five participants, so the findings show early patterns rather than results that can represent all gallery visitors.
- Participants covered several visiting styles, but the sample was small and uneven. Each profile was represented by only one person.
- The records do not provide a full recruitment protocol, interview dates, interview duration, consent procedure, or complete demographic information. These details should not be assumed.
- Questions were adapted to each participant, so not every person answered the same set of questions. Direct comparison is therefore limited.
- Contextual observations were brief and attached to the interviews. They were not a separate, systematic observation study.
- Responses describe participant preferences and intended behaviour. They do not prove how people would use a working prototype in practice.
- The parent-and-child context introduces additional privacy and age-related needs. More focused research would be required before treating families as a primary audience.
- This document reports only the interview evidence present in the source material. It does not add survey results, further observations, or new interviews.
