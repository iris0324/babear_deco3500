## Project Context

The DECO3500 project explored **Increasing Art Gallery Involvement through Social and Mobile Computing (SOMO)**. The problem space focused on how visitors engage with artworks, other visitors, and the gallery environment.

The project started with the idea that gallery visits can be passive and individual. A visitor may look at an artwork briefly, feel unsure about its meaning, and move on. Social and mobile technology may support a richer experience, but it can also create problems. For example, too much screen use may take attention away from the physical artwork.

The early design question was:

> How might social and mobile technologies help art gallery visitors engage with artworks and exchange interpretations without disrupting the gallery experience?

## Stakeholders

### Primary Stakeholders — Gallery Visitors

Gallery visitors are the main stakeholders because they would directly experience the final design. The project is particularly interested in:

- visitors with limited art knowledge;
- casual gallery visitors;
- visitors attending alone; and
- visitors attending with friends or companions.

The research aimed to understand whether these differences affect visitors' confidence in interpreting art, their level of engagement, and their interest in other people's perspectives.

### Secondary Stakeholders

**Gallery educators and learning staff** can explain how galleries currently support visitor learning and interpretation.

**Visitor experience and engagement staff** can provide insight into visitor behaviour, accessibility, and how a new interaction could fit into a normal gallery visit.

**Gallery guides and volunteers** may notice common visitor questions, confusion, and behaviours that are difficult to identify through literature alone.

**Curators and interpretation staff** can help the team understand how visitor-generated interpretations could exist alongside formal curatorial information.

**Digital experience staff** can provide advice about feasibility, accessibility, maintenance, and how a digital interaction could work within the physical gallery.

These groups were identified as relevant stakeholders. The available research record does not show that secondary stakeholders were interviewed, so their expected contributions are presented as future research opportunities rather than completed findings.

## Research Objectives

The research aimed to understand:

1. how casual visitors currently view and interpret artworks;
2. what makes an artwork difficult or easy to understand;
3. whether visitors are interested in other people's interpretations;
4. how comfortable visitors feel about talking to strangers in a gallery;
5. how visitors use mobile phones during a gallery visit;
6. when digital support is helpful or distracting; and
7. what could encourage visitors to spend more time with an artwork.

These objectives were intended to test the team's early assumptions and guide the later design process.

## Participants and Recruitment

### Intended participants

The initial target group was **casual or inexperienced gallery visitors**. These visitors may be interested in art but may not have formal art knowledge. This audience was an early assumption from domain and literature research, not a final user definition validated through fieldwork.

### Planned recruitment

The proposed approach was short intercept recruitment using convenience sampling. Potential participants would be approached in public areas around the university or other nearby public spaces. They would be asked if they were willing to join a short interview about art gallery experiences.

The plan was to include people with different levels of gallery experience rather than only art or design experts.

### Actual recruitment

Six people took part in the interviews. The participant descriptions and responses used in the analysis refer to these real interviews, and audio recordings were retained. The participants included people with different ages and levels of gallery experience. The available written record does not fully preserve recruitment dates, exact locations, or consent procedures, so these details should be added only after they are checked against the recordings or project files.

## Interview Method

### Method

The project used short semi-structured interviews. The interviewer used open-ended questions and follow-up questions when a participant shared a useful experience. The original plan expected each session to take about five to ten minutes; the exact duration of each recorded interview should be checked before it is reported as a final fact.

The interview was designed to begin with general gallery habits and then move to more focused topics:

- understanding and interpreting artworks;
- discussing art with friends, family, or other visitors;
- interest in other visitors' viewpoints;
- comfort with direct social interaction; and
- mobile phone use in galleries.

Semi-structured interviews were suitable because they could collect personal stories while keeping each conversation connected to the same research objectives.

The conversations were audio-recorded. The recordings are primary evidence and should be stored securely rather than uploaded to a public GitHub repository unless every participant gave permission. An anonymised transcript or selected de-identified quotations would be safer evidence for a public portfolio.

## Interview Questions

The following interview guide was developed for the project:

1. **How often do you visit art galleries or museums?**
2. **When you see an artwork that interests you, what do you usually do?**
3. **Have you ever liked an artwork but found it difficult to understand? What did you do?**
4. **When you visit a gallery with friends or family, do you usually talk about the artworks?**
5. **Are you interested in knowing what other visitors think about an artwork? Why or why not?**
6. **Would you feel comfortable talking directly to a stranger about an artwork? Why or why not?**
7. **Do you use your phone while visiting a gallery? How does it affect your experience?**
8. **What could make you more engaged or make you spend more time with an artwork?**

Possible follow-up prompts included:

- Can you tell me about a specific experience?
- What made you feel that way?
- What did you do next?
- Would this be different if you visited alone or with someone else?
- What kind of information or interaction would be useful?

These prompts were intended to collect examples and stories rather than short yes-or-no answers.

## Summary of Participant Responses

Six real participant interviews were completed and recorded. The response set showed patterns such as:

- uncertainty when interpreting unfamiliar or contemporary art;
- difficulty understanding long or academic gallery labels;
- interest in seeing different interpretations from other visitors;
- discomfort with starting face-to-face conversations with strangers;
- use of phones for photography, searching, QR codes, or audio; and
- concern that screen use could distract attention from the artwork.

The available conversation contains summaries of the responses. The recordings should be checked before direct quotations are published, because a portfolio should preserve the participants' exact meaning and wording.

## Thematic Analysis

The table below records the thematic analysis developed from the six real interviews. The evidence is paraphrased from the existing response summaries. Direct quotations should be checked against the audio recordings before publication.

| Theme | Evidence status and preliminary pattern | User Need | Insight | Design Implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Difficulty understanding art | Several participants described uncertainty about an artwork's meaning, what to look at, or academic gallery language. | Visitors need accessible support when exploring unfamiliar artworks. | Interest in art may decrease when visitors lack confidence or cannot access the information. | Offer short and optional prompts or explanations. Support personal interpretation instead of giving one correct answer. |
| Interest in other visitors' perspectives | Participants wanted to compare interpretations and understand why other people viewed an artwork differently. | Visitors need a way to discover a small range of meaningful viewpoints. | Another visitor's interpretation may help someone notice details or meanings they missed. | Allow visitors to share a short interpretation, reaction, or question rather than only a rating. |
| Social curiosity and social barriers | Participants showed interest in others' views but described awkwardness, uncertainty, or concern about disturbing strangers. | Visitors need low-pressure ways to feel connected with others. | Visitors may value social connection without wanting direct face-to-face interaction. | Explore optional, indirect, or asynchronous interaction such as anonymous notes, short responses, or activity traces. |
| Mobile support and mobile distraction | Participants discussed phones for photos, searches, QR codes, and audio, while also describing how screen use could move attention away from artworks. | Visitors need digital support that keeps the artwork at the centre of the experience. | Mobile technology is helpful when it supports attention, but harmful when the screen becomes the main activity. | Reduce steps and screen time. Consider audio, glanceable content, or location-specific prompts. |
| Lightweight and accessible content | Participants preferred forms such as short explanations, stories, prompts, audio, and a limited number of comments. | Visitors need clear information that they can choose and consume quickly. | More content does not always create more engagement; timing and accessibility may be more important. | Use layered content: begin with a short item and let visitors choose whether to open more detail. |

## Key Findings

The following findings were developed from the six interviews. They represent this small participant group and should not be generalised to all gallery visitors:

1. Some casual visitors may be interested in art but lack confidence in interpreting unfamiliar artworks.
2. Visitors may value other people's interpretations because different viewpoints can support reflection.
3. Visitors may want a sense of social connection while avoiding direct conversation with strangers.
4. Digital technology may support a gallery visit, but too much screen interaction may reduce attention to physical artworks.
5. Short, optional, and accessible content may be more useful than long or highly academic information.

The main hypothesis is:

> Visitors may want help understanding and discussing art through low-pressure social interaction, while keeping the physical artwork as the main focus.

The two observation visits added real contextual experience, although the incomplete records limit how strongly the observations can support individual findings.

## How Research Informed Design

The interview analysis helped the team move from a broad topic to a clearer design opportunity:

> How might we help visitors discover and exchange different interpretations of artworks through lightweight social interaction without distracting them from the physical artwork?

The analysis suggested several provisional design principles:

- support interpretation rather than provide a single correct meaning;
- allow social awareness without forcing direct conversation;
- make participation optional and low pressure;
- keep interactions brief;
- use mobile technology as background support; and
- keep the physical artwork as the main focus.

Possible concepts included short anonymous interpretations, reactions or questions connected to an artwork, asynchronous responses, audio content, and location-specific prompts. These were design directions generated from the interview analysis and gallery context. They still required prototype evaluation.

## Limitations

This research record has important limitations:

- The study involved only six interview participants.
- Audio recordings exist, but they were not available while this report was being prepared.
- Full transcripts, consent records, recruitment notes, dates, and locations were not available in the current document set.
- The intended audience was based mainly on early literature and team assumptions.
- No survey was conducted.
- Two observation visits were completed, but the surviving evidence is incomplete.
- The themes may have been influenced by the wording and order of the interview questions.
- The themes cannot be generalised to all gallery visitors.
- Design ideas based on these themes require further research and evaluation.

To improve the study, the recordings should be transcribed and anonymised, observation memories should be reconstructed separately by each team member, and all claims should be linked to a transcript line, field note, photograph, or clearly labelled recollection. Future sessions should also preserve consent records, dates, locations, and coding decisions.

## Reflection

This work helped me understand that user research is not only about writing questions or collecting answers. A researcher must identify repeated experiences, values, and problems, and then explain how these patterns can guide design.

The thematic analysis helped me follow the path from **response to theme, user need, insight, and design implication**. It also showed me why research evidence must be stored carefully. The interviews produced useful evidence, but incomplete observation records made it difficult to show the full process later.

If I continued this research, I would ask more follow-up questions about specific gallery experiences. This would produce richer stories and reduce the risk of leading participants toward the team's early ideas. I would also create a shared observation template before every site visit and save field notes immediately afterwards. Finally, I would document recruitment, consent, interview transcripts, coding decisions, and changes to the design so that the process could be checked by other team members.
