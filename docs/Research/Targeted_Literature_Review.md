# Targeted Literature Review

## Overview

After developing **Borrowed Eyes**, we conducted additional targeted research to better understand the interaction used in the concept.

Our earlier research focused on:

- visitor confidence;
- artwork interpretation;
- social interaction;
- mobile technology;
- attention to physical artworks.

As the concept developed, we also needed to understand:

- close looking;
- curiosity and play;
- visitor-to-visitor learning;
- social awareness;
- shared attention;
- mobile-mediated social interaction;
- location-aware museum experiences.

This research helps connect our design decisions to existing museum and HCI research.

---

# 1. Close Looking and Active Observation

One important part of Borrowed Eyes is the use of a cropped visual detail.

Visitors receive a small part of an artwork and search for the complete artwork in the physical gallery.

The purpose is not only to create a challenge. The search should also encourage visitors to look more carefully.

Timonen (2025) studied **slow looking** in museums. The study suggests that longer observation can help visitors notice details and develop personal responses to artworks.

Steier et al. (2015) also showed that interpretation in art museums involves:

- observation;
- movement;
- body position;
- gesture;
- interaction with others.

This means that interpretation is not only about receiving information.

### Design Relevance

Borrowed Eyes does not begin with the artwork title or an expert explanation.

Instead, it begins with:

> **Visual Detail → Clue → Search**

The visual detail gives visitors a starting point.

The search then encourages them to move through the gallery and examine physical artworks.

### Design Hypothesis

> Searching for a visual detail may encourage closer observation of physical artworks.

This should be tested because the search could also become only a guessing task.

---

# 2. Curiosity and Play in Museums

Borrowed Eyes includes a game-like search activity.

Visitors:

1. choose a visual fragment;
2. receive a clue;
3. search for the artwork;
4. discover where the fragment belongs.

Grace D et al. (2025) studied games and interactive design in museums.

Their research discusses how play can support:

- curiosity;
- discovery;
- exploration;
- active participation.

However, museum games should connect meaningfully to the museum experience.

### Design Relevance

Borrowed Eyes is not designed as a treasure hunt where finding the correct artwork is the final goal.

The search is used to create curiosity.

It gives visitors a reason to approach and examine artworks that they may otherwise walk past.

For this reason, the current concept does not focus on:

- points;
- rankings;
- rewards;
- competition.

The main goal is still observation and interpretation.

---

# 3. Visitor-to-Visitor Learning

Borrowed Eyes is not completely created by the design team or gallery.

Visitors also create content for future visitors.

Pitts (2018) discusses **visitor-to-visitor learning** in museums.

The research shows how open-ended activities can support visitor-directed inquiry without requiring continuous support from museum educators.

Visitors can therefore contribute to experiences that later support other visitors.

### Design Relevance

In Borrowed Eyes, one visitor can create:

> **Visual Detail + Clue + Perspective**

Another visitor later receives this content.

The next visitor must still:

- search for the artwork;
- observe it;
- form their own interpretation;
- compare perspectives.

They can then create something for another visitor.

This creates a chain:

> **Visitor A notices → Visitor B explores → Visitor B contributes → Visitor C explores**

The experience can therefore grow through visitor participation.

---

# 4. Other Visitors' Perspectives and Reflection

Other visitors' perspectives can support museum meaning-making.

Yi et al. (2022) found that visitor-based social contextual information can allow visitors to encounter and compare different responses to museum objects.

Vom Lehn (2018) also examined how social interaction and discourse can shape:

- cultural participation;
- aesthetic evaluation;
- meaning-making.

Other people's observations can affect what visitors notice and how they respond.

### Design Relevance

Borrowed Eyes does not show another visitor's interpretation immediately.

The visitor first:

> **Finds → Observes → Forms their own response**

Then another visitor's perspective becomes visible.

The visitor can compare it with their own response.

The intended interaction is:

> **My View → Other View → Compare → Look Again**

We treat this sequence as a **design hypothesis**.

The literature supports the value of other perspectives, but it does not prove that this exact interaction will work.

---

# 5. Low-Pressure and Indirect Social Interaction

Social museum experiences do not always require direct conversation.

Borrowed Eyes explores **indirect and asynchronous social interaction**.

Visitors do not need to:

- know each other;
- meet each other;
- start a conversation with a stranger.

Instead, they encounter another visitor through what that person noticed and left behind.

### Design Relevance

The interaction can happen across time:

> **Previous Visitor → Current Visitor → Future Visitor**

This creates a low-pressure form of social participation.

However, we should not assume that anonymous interaction automatically creates a meaningful social connection.

### Design Question

> Can indirect visitor-generated content create awareness or curiosity about another visitor?

This needs to be tested through the prototype.

---

# 6. Keeping Attention on the Physical Artwork

Mobile technology can support museum experiences, but it can also compete with physical exhibits.

Sung et al. (2010) found that electronic guides can compete with exhibits for visitors' attention.

Stanković Elesini et al. (2022) also discussed problems such as:

- head-down behaviour;
- isolation;
- reduced conversation.

However, digital activities connected to the physical museum environment can also support engagement.

### Design Relevance

The phone should not become the main experience.

Borrowed Eyes should move attention between:

> **Phone → Physical Artwork → Phone → Physical Artwork**

The interface provides:

- visual fragments;
- clues;
- visitor perspectives;
- contribution tools.

But the physical gallery remains necessary for completing the experience.

This leads to an important design principle:

> **Use the phone to direct attention back to the artwork.**

---

# 7. Social Awareness and Shared Attention

Borrowed Eyes is becoming more social in the next design iteration.

It is therefore important to understand how visitors become aware of other people in gallery spaces.

Christidou (2018) studied **joint attention** between visitors in an art museum.

The research shows that gallery visits can involve coordination between people and artworks.

Visitors may move their attention between:

- the artwork;
- another visitor;
- a detail noticed by another person.

Shared attention can therefore become part of the artwork experience.

### Design Relevance

Borrowed Eyes can make another visitor's attention visible.

For example, the system could show:

- what detail another visitor noticed;
- which artwork another visitor explored;
- different perspectives around the same artwork.

The purpose is not to tell visitors what they should think.

Instead, the system can create awareness:

> **Someone else looked here too.**

This may encourage visitors to compare different ways of looking.

### Design Question

> Can awareness of another visitor's attention create a stronger social experience around the artwork?

This should be tested in the next prototype.

---

# 8. Mobile-Mediated Social Interaction

Mobile devices do not automatically create social interaction.

In some cases, they can reduce interaction.

Lanir et al. (2013) studied a location-aware mobile museum guide with real museum visitors.

The study found that mobile guides changed visitor behaviour.

It also found a possible **decoupling effect**. Visitors using mobile guides could become less connected to the people visiting with them.

Other museum research has explored how mobile experiences can instead support group and visitor-to-visitor interaction.

Katifori et al. (2016) explored group-based mobile storytelling in museums.

Their work suggests that mobile technology can support visitor-to-visitor engagement when social interaction is designed as part of the experience.

### Design Relevance

This creates an important challenge for Borrowed Eyes.

We should not simply move the paper prototype onto a phone.

The mobile prototype should create meaningful connections between visitors.

Possible interactions include:

- seeing another visitor's perspective;
- responding to another visitor;
- exchanging visual attention;
- looking at an artwork together;
- leaving content for future visitors.

The mobile interface should support the social experience, not replace it.

### Design Principle

> **Mobile interaction should connect Visitor → Artwork → Visitor.**

---

# 9. Location-Aware and Contextual Museum Experiences

Mobile computing can connect digital information to the visitor's physical location.

Lanir et al. (2013) studied a **location-aware mobile museum guide**.

The study found that location-aware mobile information can influence:

- where visitors go;
- which exhibits they visit;
- how long they spend at exhibits.

However, location-aware systems can also change social behaviour.

This means location should be used carefully.

### Design Relevance

Borrowed Eyes could use gallery location as part of the interaction.

For example:

> **You are near this artwork.**

or:

> **Other visitors have left perspectives in this gallery area.**

The system does not need to show every artwork or every visitor contribution.

Instead, it can provide content connected to the visitor's current physical context.

This supports the relationship between:

> **Mobile Context → Physical Location → Artwork → Other Visitors**

### Design Question

> Can location-aware content make Borrowed Eyes feel more connected to the physical gallery?

This can be explored in the mid-fidelity prototype.

---

# 10. Research Synthesis

The targeted literature helped us identify several connected design principles.

| Research Area | Key Idea | Borrowed Eyes Design Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Close Looking | Longer and more active observation can support interpretation | Search using visual fragments |
| Curiosity & Play | Play can encourage exploration when connected to museum content | Clues and discovery |
| Visitor-to-Visitor Learning | Visitors can contribute to other visitors' experiences | Visitor-generated clues and perspectives |
| Other Perspectives | Different viewpoints can support reflection | Compare perspectives after own interpretation |
| Low-Pressure Social Interaction | Social experiences do not always require direct conversation | Anonymous and asynchronous sharing |
| Physical Artwork Attention | Mobile devices can distract from exhibits | Use the phone to return attention to the artwork |
| Social Awareness | Other people's attention can shape how artworks are experienced | Show what other visitors noticed |
| Mobile Social Interaction | Mobile devices can support or reduce social interaction | Design explicit visitor-to-visitor interaction |
| Location Awareness | Digital content can respond to physical context | Connect visitor traces to nearby artworks |

---

# 11. Updated Design Direction

The literature suggests that Borrowed Eyes should not become only a digital gallery guide.

The experience should connect three elements:

> **Visitor ↔ Artwork ↔ Other Visitors**

The next mid-fidelity prototype will therefore explore:

### 1. Social Awareness

Visitors can become aware that other people have interacted with the same artwork.

### 2. Perspective Exchange

Visitors can see and respond to different ways of looking.

### 3. Shared Attention

One visitor's observation can guide another visitor's attention.

### 4. Look Again

The interface encourages visitors to return their attention to the physical artwork.

### 5. Visitor Contribution

Visitors can leave a visual detail, clue, or perspective for another visitor.

### 6. Context-Aware Interaction

Digital content can be connected to the visitor's current artwork or gallery area.

The updated interaction can be described as:

> **Discover → Observe → Interpret → See Others → React → Look Again → Contribute**

These ideas are design directions, not confirmed findings.

They should be evaluated through the next mid-fidelity prototype.
