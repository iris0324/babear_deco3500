# Borrowed Eyes — Survey Findings

## Purpose
The survey explored gallery visitors' preferences for individual and social participation, motivations for discovering other perspectives, and perceived barriers to the proposed Blind Eyes and Face-to-Face activities. It also examined participation conditions and possible ways to join an activity.

## Key Findings

### Finding 1 — Visitors prefer flexible social interaction
**Evidence:** 21 of 30 respondents (70%) enjoyed looking at artworks quietly; only 9 of 30 (30%) felt confident sharing their interpretations with other visitors.

**Interpretation:** Interest in art does not necessarily imply comfort with public discussion. Social engagement should be a choice rather than a compulsory step.

**Design implication:** Retain a Solo option and provide participation modes with different degrees of interaction.

### Finding 2 — Discovering new perspectives is a key motivation
**Evidence:** When asked about Blind Eyes, 15 of 30 respondents (50%) were interested in noticing overlooked details, and 13 of 30 (43%) wanted to understand how others viewed artworks.

**Interpretation:** The anticipated value of the activity extends beyond identifying the correct artwork.

**Design implication:** Keep perspective discovery and closer observation central to the experience, rather than rewarding search completion alone.

### Finding 3 — Anonymity alone does not eliminate barriers
**Evidence:** 10 of 30 respondents (33%) felt anonymity would make participation more comfortable; 13 of 30 (43%) were concerned that a partner might not respond; 11 of 30 (37%) were concerned about communication difficulties.

**Interpretation:** Anonymous interaction may still involve uncertainty and communication friction. These figures do not establish anonymity's causal effect on comfort.

**Design implication:** Explain communication rules, provide optional prompts, and consider how to respond if a partner is unresponsive.

### Finding 4 — Face-to-Face collaboration can create social pressure
**Evidence:** 8 of 30 respondents (27%) felt comfortable participating with unfamiliar visitors, and 14 of 30 (47%) worried that one person might dominate the discussion.

**Interpretation:** Simply forming a group does not guarantee balanced contributions.

**Design implication:** Give members complementary clues and test whether quieter participants have meaningful opportunities to contribute.

### Finding 5 — Clear goals and predictable activities support willingness to join
**Evidence:** 15 of 30 respondents (50%) valued clear goals, and 22 of 30 (73%) wanted to know the duration before starting.

**Interpretation:** A predictable structure may reduce uncertainty about participation.

**Design implication:** State the goal, expected duration, group size, and how to stop or leave the activity.

### Finding 6 — Group chat may not be the best default entry point
**Evidence:** Only 2 of 30 respondents (7%) preferred joining through group chat; 11 of 30 (37%) preferred joining with friends or family.

**Interpretation:** A group-chat-first onboarding approach is not well supported by this sample.

**Design implication:** Explore direct entry at the gallery, an interactive station, or joining with existing companions. Test alternatives rather than assuming one is better.

### Finding 7 — Meaningful teamwork requires opportunities to contribute
**Evidence:** Open-ended responses to Q19 emphasised a shared goal, building on others' observations, and allowing contributions without requiring everyone to speak extensively.

**Interpretation:** Collaboration should not be measured merely by whether people are talking or standing together.

**Design implication:** Use complementary information and prompts that invite each participant's perspective without demanding equal speaking time.

## Synthesis
The survey indicates variation in visitors' willingness to engage socially. It supports exploring Solo, Blind Eyes, and Face-to-Face as different participation options, but it does **not** validate the usability or effectiveness of these modes. The findings identify potential barriers in communication, coordination, and cooperation and challenge the assumption that group chat is the preferred way to form teams.

## Limitations
- The sample included 30 respondents; the recruitment approach and demographic representativeness are not established in the supplied summary.
- All quantitative findings reflect self-report rather than observed actions in a gallery.
- Some percentages relate to separate survey questions and should not be combined or interpreted as mutually exclusive categories.
- The supplied summary does not include the complete questionnaire, raw response file, or response-level analysis. Further statistical claims are not justified.

## Suggested next steps
1. Test a clearly presented choice of participation modes.
2. Compare onboarding approaches without assuming group chat is preferred.
3. Observe whether different clues result in actual contribution and mutual understanding.
4. Measure participation comfort and voice, especially for quieter group members.
